A cliff, a ledge, a few steps of momentum. And that hesitation before you press the button. To tell the story of the 30 years of Tomb Raider, you could start with the numbers, the films, or the various interpretations of Lara Croft. Yet the heart of it all lies in a very simple memory: spotting a place that seems out of reach and figuring out how to get there.

Originally, Tomb Raider is an action-adventure game developed by Core Design and published by Eidos Interactive. At the Derby studios in England, a small team brought to life the project conceived by Toby Gard, the creator of Lara Croft. His influences include Indiana Jones, Prince of Persia, and John Woo’s action cinema. The aim was bold: to blend exploration, acrobatics, puzzles, and shootouts in fully three-dimensional environments.

In 1996, that third dimension still opens up possibilities developers are learning to master. With Tomb Raider, it becomes a terrain to master. A room unfolds in its depth, a balcony glimpsed from afar hints at a passage, a pool may conceal an exit. The player must observe, backtrack, and sometimes linger before a mechanism whose logic eludes him. The environments demand as much imagination as they do reflexes.

This freedom comes with a price. Lara moves with a certain stiffness, jumps demand precision, and the camera can complicate things. Yet movement follows a coherent system, built on the grid that underpins the environments. Gradually, you learn the distances the way you would learn the capabilities of your own body. Veteran developers point out how this structure shaped the animations and the level design. Successfully clearing a section yields a distinct satisfaction: you’ve figured out how to do it.

The first episode also has its surprises. In the Lost Valley, a Tyrannosaurus bursts onto the scene, turning exploration into instant panic. Elsewhere, the monumental rooms, traps, and submerged passages cultivate a creeping sense of unease. The score leaves room for footsteps and the waiting. Tomb Raider cultivates this blend of curiosity and apprehension: the desire to open the next door, tempered by fear of what lies behind it.

At the heart of this adventure, a woman leads the charge. Lara Croft knows how to fight, to read a situation, and to talk her way out of trouble. She exudes a nearly defiant, self-assured aura that contrasts with the desolation surrounding her. In an era when action-game icons are mostly male, her presence matters. Samus Aran had already shown with Metroid that a woman could carry an adventure, and Lara gives that representation a fresh, instantly identifiable face, voice, and personality for a wide audience.

Her success, however, comes with an ambiguity that runs through the entire series. Exaggerated proportions, outfits, and promotional poses turn her body into a selling point. We can admire her independence while also watching how she’s staged to be alluring. These two readings coexist, sometimes within the same person. A female gamer may see Lara as a figure of power; a teenager might be first drawn by her looks, then spend hours solving her puzzles. The reception of a character always escapes a bit from the intentions of those who commercialize her.

That's what makes Lara so fascinating: her role gradually transcends the public image surrounding her. We remember her prowess, her composure, the way she strides into places where no one should go alone. She becomes a character you recognize even if you’ve never played her adventures.

The series is speeding up. Tomb Raider II hits shelves in 1997, Tomb Raider III in 1998, The Last Revelation in 1999, and Chronicles of Lara Croft in 2000. Destinations and scenarios multiply, as a new installment becomes an almost annual deadline.
