While video game adaptations seem to be materializing at an alarming rate in recent years, they've been happening within the TV and movie industry for decades. Some are hailed as masterpieces, others are heavily criticized, and an unfortunate number are instantly swept aside for how bland and unmemorable they are. However, almost three decades ago, one video game adaptation became an overnight sensation, and it didn't just rely on the fans' love for the source material.

Instead, it focused on making the best screen-based version of the story it possibly could. In 2026, no other video game adaptation has taken the crown that the show in question immediately inherited upon release. The weirdest part of the entire scenario is that the project is almost never included in the conversation of what the best video game adaptations of all time are. This, at least to me, feels like a massive injustice, and it's time we talked about how great it was – and still is.

More recent video game adaptations like The Last of Us and Fallout have been hailed as among the best within the genre, and they probably still are. However, because many of these modern, gritty, adult-centered projects have been so successful, it can be easy to forget some of the more fun, child-friendly adaptations like Pokémon. The thing is, the anime doesn't get enough credit for how great it is when it comes to how it adapts the games. Some even forget that the games came first at all.

In the US, the anime came out just ahead of the games' international release.

First airing in Japan in 1997, Pokémon boasted such a convincing and detailed world that the fan base suddenly ballooned. The first games landed in 1996, and all the hard work that had gone into the groundbreaking project quickly triggered a franchise expansion that included manga, trading cards, and the aforementioned anime series. While the other areas of the franchise are still successful, the ongoing animated Pokémon adventures just about remain the flagship property. Of course, popularity doesn't always equal quality, but that is the case with Pokémon. The anime was a trailblazer for video game adaptations when it came out, and nothing has pushed the envelope as much since.

As great and as popular as the first Pokémon games are, they were still incredibly new when the anime came out around a year later. As such, the fan base had no strong opinions on how the show should feel and look. Besides, younger viewers tend to be less discerning in this respect, which has probably played a big part in the freedom the show's writers have enjoyed. In the modern age, there can sometimes be an obsessive desire to have a video game adaptation follow the source material to the letter, but that doesn't always work too well. For Pokémon, that approach would probably have been disastrous.

Thankfully, the anime only gently honored the elements of the games that wouldn't have made sense on the small screen if they had been strictly adhered to. For instance, the leveling up of the Pokémon wasn't meticulously tracked. Instead, they just gradually became stronger and more experienced in the anime until they evolved. They were probably going through the same sort of minute progressions as they were in the games, but that could either be accepted or denied depending on who was watching. The source material's turn-based combat element did largely remain in place for the anime, but it was portrayed with much more fluidity than how it worked in the games, allowing the Pokémon battles to feel more tense and cinematic.

Furthermore, the show refused to canonize a specific playing experience that might have been enjoyed by someone tuning in. The Gen 1 games offered up the iconic choice of Charmander, Squirtle, or Bulbasaur as the player's starter Pokémon, but the anime tastefully made this impossible for Ash Ketchum. He was paired with a rebellious Pikachu instead.
